01 · The Pass By

Inside the satellite belt

375 meters of rock, moving at 7.4 km per second, threading the ring where our weather and communication satellites orbit. An object this size comes this close roughly once every 5,000 to 10,000 years. This time half the planet can watch it with the naked eye.

03 · The name

Named for the end of the world

In Egyptian myth, Apophis is the serpent of chaos who tries to swallow the sun every night, and every night is held back. Astronomers chose the name in 2004, when this rock looked like it might be the real thing.

The ancient story ends the same way ours does. The light holds.

04 · Where to see it

The best seats on Earth

Apophis is visible to the naked eye across Europe, Africa, the Middle East and western Asia. The best views are where it passes high in a dark, dry sky, ideally while it's still up at closest approach. North America sees almost nothing. Our site is still to be announced.

06 · Questions

Questions

Is Apophis going to hit Earth?

No. Radar measurements in 2021 ruled out any impact in 2029 and for at least the next 100 years. It passes about 31,900 km above the surface and heads back out into space.

Will I be able to see it?

Yes, with clear, dark skies and no telescope. It looks like a bright star, about as bright as the Big Dipper, moving steadily across the sky. Binoculars and telescopes on site show it sliding against the stars in real time.

When exactly is it?

Friday 13 April 2029. Apophis is brightest around 20:35 UTC and closest at 21:45 UTC. The Moon is new that night, so the sky is as dark as it gets.

Where is the gathering?

To be announced. The full pass is visible from Europe, Africa and the Middle East, and we're choosing a site with dry air, dark skies and Apophis nearly overhead. Join the list to hear first.

Why not watch from home?

From North America you'll barely see it. Elsewhere, city lights and clouds can hide a star-like object. And a once-in-thousands-of-years moment is better shared.

Are spacecraft going there?

Yes. ESA's Ramses arrives before the flyby and rides along with Apophis through it. NASA's OSIRIS-APEX arrives afterward to study how Earth's gravity changed it.

Who is behind this?

IMXP, the team behind Iceland Eclipse and the Eclipse Trilogy, building immersive gatherings around the sky's biggest moments.
